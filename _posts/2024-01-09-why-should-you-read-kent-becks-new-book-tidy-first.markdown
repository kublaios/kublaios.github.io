*Assuming you want to be on the winning side when your imperatives clash with others.*

*This post was originally published on [Sigma Newsletter](https://sigmanewsletter.substack.com/p/why-should-you-read-kent-becks-new). Consider subscribing there for my content about growth and productivity.*

You have to understand how valuable your time is. This is often an easy conclusion for two reasons: People will keep telling you about it (like I just did), and you experience it around the clock. However, as a paid software programmer, acknowledging the ~~value~~ cost of your time for your employer will still take a while. At least that was my case.

Once that *clicked*, many things in my job got easier. I realized that maybe refactoring the `VollmilchChocolateController` was not that important at that moment, or migrating to the latest version of `TeaPotSDK` for a few convenience APIs wasn't as urgent or necessary. I also realized that I was more generous with my time than I should have been (as a responsible knowledge worker).

There is a very simple yet effective trick that the marketing departments of automation tools adore: You take an average salary and divide it until you get the dollars-per-minute rate. Almost always, it is way higher than the cost of your tool. Formulate the difference dramatically, and there you have your sales pitch.

The next time you are about to autonomously (read: without consulting anyone or asking for permission) start working on something *geeky*, do the same exercise for *your* cost per minute. Then, estimate the effort for the thing and multiply it by two (the golden rule of estimations: always multiply by two). X equals how much you burn per minute times the minutes required for the thing. Now, ask yourself this question: Do I really want to spend *my (!)* $X on this?

You do, don't you? *You old fool.* Well, I don't know what can make you give up on what you set out to do, but I do know there is a way of doing it more effectively and consciously. And, most importantly, economically.

## Tidy First? - A Personal Exercise in Empirical Software Design

![Tidy First? book cover](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F98ad7985-a5ef-433d-8674-2587f852f77f_1000x750.png)

We (programmers) are terrible at economic thinking (exceptions happen to occur). As the book states, *what makes sense for us to do as programmers may go contrary to the nature of money. When geeky imperatives clash with money imperatives, money wins. Eventually.* So, how do we get to the winning side? That's the question this book systematically answers.

There's more to this book than just the economics, though. It will help you become your best self by teaching the means of gradually improving the quality of your work. It will make you feel the joy of determination behind the little choices you make during coding. You will learn to speculate better in the face of ambiguity when designing software.

I will involve takeaways from this book many more times in my writing, but I feel I've said enough to recommend it. Go grab it and see what I mean for yourself. Chances are it will take you with the storm it has taken me (I've read it twice within 24 hours). Either way, if you feel like talking about it to someone, do me a favor and drop a comment below.

Thank you, [Kent Beck](https://substack.com/@kentbeck), for publishing this great piece of work and *helping us geeks feel safe in the world*. Here's his Substack if you're not subscribed to: [Tidy First?](https://tidyfirst.substack.com/)

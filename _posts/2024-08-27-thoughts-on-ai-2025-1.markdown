If there's one thing we've been all doing when it comes to AI, it's talking about it. We talk about how we are amazed (or amused, or terrified) by the answers ChatGPT is giving to our prompts, how we get some mundane task done within minutes thanks to Claude (or the opposite after hours of back-and-forth), whether and when companies will replace their workforce using AI, and so on.

I'm hoping you will attest that these conversations had become quite repetitive. Maybe it's the progress of AI that's stalling, or that our perception of it maturing. Regardless, I thought it would be fun to capture regularly how I see the state and future of AI so that when the time comes, I can reflect on how off my predictions were.

## Is AI going to take our jobs?

Figured I should talk about the spicy stuff first. Do I think that we will be out of jobs because of AI? Well, yes and no. Let me explain...

(Isn't this line just the perfect place for a paywall?)

*Before going any further, I want to come clean about one thing: When saying "AI", what I'm actually referring to is the large language models (LLMs). This is on purpose, because all the recent hype is stemming from the advancements made in this front. I won't go into whether I stand with folks who think [LLMs are not true AI](https://ml-site.cdn-apple.com/papers/the-illusion-of-thinking.pdf), nor with the bunch who are waiting for the day when LLMs achieve artificial general intelligence ([AGI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_general_intelligence)). I am just keeping things simple for now.*

AI (LLMs!) will take our jobs but no different than how any type of automation had in history. In a sense, AI has been taking away jobs since its day 0 (let's assume it's when GPT was made available for business customers). But it's only doing so by *doing more with less*, not necessarily with zero human intelligence. It will, however, elevate more professions and professionals to the operator seat, involving less humans for the same amount of work. If this all sounds too simple, because it is.

This will lead to

- More generalists than specialists
- Fierce competition in the market
- Less tolerance for lack of tenure

Which will result in

- Overall reduced quality in software (as if it hasn't been nosediving for years)
- Increased number of software solutions (competition at the product level, not individual)
- (Depending on how *free* markets are) big companies winning big (small fish can't survive) or losing big (small fish can move rapidly and harvest the sun before it sets)

In summary, I expect more people building software but less people making money from it, software quality taking a bit hit, and opportunities for small players arise only in specific markets/conditions (not at the global scale).

This is just my view on economics for white collars like me. Obviously this technology is getting more ubiquitous, fast, and economics might not be where it makes the biggest impact (although it currently seems to be).

Finally, I want to put emphasis on the quality piece, because if there's a single thing I see somewhat faulty in the nature of LLMs, it's how they are creating a (vicious) cycle for reinforcing AI-generated content with the content they, well, generate. I'm sure there are efforts to detect AI artifacts to exclude from training data but if a technology is the poster child for replacing humans, I expect that its output is measured by how human-like it works (therefore passing *"are you human?"* checks). Because the market incentives are asking for quick and scrappy solutions and LLMs are not going to bother changing that (contrary to their *DNA*), they will just contribute to the dominance of imperfection, if not mediocracy.

Read on if you are interested in my observations of LLMs' practical use (in my case, coding).

## AI in action

### Where do I stand?

I, an experienced iOS developer with a bit of full-stack background, currently have experience with

- Google Gemini for work-related inquiries (*write me some code that does ...*)
- GitHub Copilot for coding (*complete the code I started*)
- Google Gemini CLI Agent for personal projects (*build me an app that will summarize given YouTube video*)
- Claude Sonnet, Google Gemini, and ChatGPT for everyday questions (*tell me if it's safe eating this mushroom*)

I have limited experience with other *agents* like Claude Desktop/Code which is about to change (looking forward to that).

### How do I *feel* about (practical use of) AI?

I don't think you came here expecting to see some metrics. No, this is all about *[vibes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibe_coding)* (pun intended). My evidence is anectodal and success is measured based on whether I could actually complete a task using AI, how much friction I had while doing so, how my velocity felt like, and how it paid off in the mid-term (was the process repeatable, did AI miss some things and I had to pay the price later on, etc.). 

I will categorize my observations based on two use cases: Developer mode (where I initiate the work and let AI fill in the blanks) and founder mode (where AI takes the wheel and I audit its work). This should be all the structure we need for now.

#### Developer mode

This is the main use case for me where I either use AI as autocompletion on steroids or for asking for chunks of code based on requirements I specify.

When in this mode, chances are equal that AI can be impressive, unimpressive, and disappointing. It's because depending on how I'm prompting it, I might already have the code written in my head but just don't want to do the typing. If AI is giving me the code I need, I am impressed in case of medium/high complexity or it was already table stakes for the opposite. When it begins to disappoint though, is when it begins to lack nuance (depending on how sophisticated the code is) or even can't *see* what's in front of it (say, I write the first line and the second line is a simple permutation of it, but AI spits out something completely off - a variant of *[hallucination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallucination_(artificial_intelligence))*).

Based on all these, could I live without it? Yes. Does it make me more effective? Absolutely.

#### Founder mode

I call this the founder mode because it roughly goes like "I have an idea and need you to build it". This is where I have the least experience and most skepticism. Regardless of which mode I'm in, I want to be acquainted with the code as if it's mine. In this mode, you have the option to approve every iteration AI makes but in my opinion, this is an illusion. The pace of the *agent* (AI) overwhelms the *operator* (human) quickly, and (more often than not) unnecessarily. The agent (in my case, Gemini CLI) works almost as if it's copy/pasting relevant Stack Overflow answers until it's *confident* that the code works. As you can imagine, this can easily become an endless loop of trial and error and impossible to keep up.

I'm sure this workflow can be improved (I want to believe so) and I am willing to try. First option comes to mind is being more detailed and prescriptive, although it would conflict with the *founder* notion. I am also remotely familiar with providing more upfront context/configuration for these agents but will need to spend some time with them before forming an opinion.

Finally, AI agents may be interesting concepts for non-coders (hence the founder mode) but I want to stay involved with code. I don't want the agent to take it away from me, that's why I've been using the developer mode happily. It doesn't mean there are times where I need more *agentic* capabilities (like a simple *"find me where this type was used that way"* query) so I am also ready to step into this direction.

#### Pitfalls

I have grown biased for AI in certain use cases and it pays off in the short term. If I know what AI is not capable of, I don't waste my time trying. This can be counterintuitive though in case it closes the gap at some point but I continue neglecting it. Something I need to keep myself under check.

Another pitfall is how my brain is wired for the AI code coming after my cursor once I type a few letters. Lately I've been having connectivity issues and Copilot becoming occasionally unavailable makes me feel more in control than just following up on how AI finishes what I started. Because otherwise, I feel like I do the thinking too late, insufficiently, and driven by AI's response (biased). With larger pieces of code this becomes even a bigger issue. What seems to help is do the upfront thinking for at least one piece of production code/test code duo. For example, writing the test cases myself so that if both me and AI misses a spot, it is still caught by the younger version of me (who'd have thought older doesn't always mean wiser?)

## Final words

AI is a difficult topic to cover and will not get any easier. I am glad to have a starting point so I can expand on bits and pieces of it later on. I agree with the sentiment that AI is just a tool and should be treated like other tools but I also find it interesting to stretch our thoughts a bit and see if there's more ground to cover. I hope I made it clear that I don't think it will replace human intelligence, although I still have a few more words to say on that topic. Until then, stay tuned.
